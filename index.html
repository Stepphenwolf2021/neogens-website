01 — The problem

The institutions that invented knowledge management are being left behind by organisations holding a fraction of their material.

Museums, libraries and archives built this discipline. Cataloguing, authority control, provenance, the practice of separating an attribution from a certainty — all of it existed in these buildings for two centuries before anyone in industry said “data governance”.

They are now at risk of becoming the least modern institutions in a society whose knowledge they hold.

The asset

They are sitting on the best material anyone has

Real objects with documented provenance. Primary sources. Field notes. A century of scholarship. Specialists who have spent decades inside one collection. None of it can be licensed, scraped or generated.

The standing

And on trust nobody else still commands

A visitor arrives assuming the institution is telling them the truth. Almost no other organisation in public life still enjoys that assumption — and it took a century of careful practice to earn.

The exposure

Both are locked in a form technology can't reach

Catalogue records, conservation files, exhibition scripts, published papers, and the memories of people approaching retirement. Rich, correct, and structurally unable to reach a modern audience — or a machine.

This is not a story about budgets or about staff. It is a story about form. The knowledge is there and it is better than anyone else's. It was simply never written down in a shape that the systems now mediating public attention can work with.

What we propose
Take the leadership back — on the merits, not on nostalgia.

Modern Knowledge Management is the practice of turning an institution's domain knowledge into a managed asset that creates value for the organisation and for its audience — by designing an ontology and knowledge graph genuinely aligned to that institution's philosophy and mission, rather than to a generic template.

Done properly it holds not only what is known, but how it came to be known — which is what makes it safe to put AI on top of, and what turns a fixed collection into something that can answer questions it was never designed for.

The ambition is not survival. These institutions were the world's knowledge leaders and the underlying advantage has never actually gone away. The work is to make that advantage usable again, and to reclaim that position on the strength of the assets they already hold.

02 — What Modern Knowledge Management means

The difference is one layer — and where you put it.

Most organisations connect AI straight to their documents and hope for the best. The model reads what it's given and produces the most plausible answer. It works, right up until the moment the answer matters.

Modern Knowledge Management puts a layer in between: an explicit model of the things you deal in, and a graph of how they relate — each statement carrying its source. The AI then works on that, instead of guessing.

So what is actually in there?

Less exotic than it sounds. It's the diagram anyone would draw on a whiteboard to explain how things in your institution relate — written down in a form a machine can follow.

Old knowledge management

Store it so a person can find it

Repositories, folder structures, search. Optimised for retrieval by a human who already knows roughly what they're looking for.

What changed

A machine now answers first

People ask a model before they open a system. Whatever the model can't verify, it invents — fluently, and in your institution's name.

Modern knowledge management

Structure it so a machine can be held to it

Explicit meaning, explicit relationships, explicit evidence. Built so the answer can be traced, repeated and defended.

03 — Why "we already have all of this" isn't enough

Your knowledge exists. It just can't be traversed.

The usual objection is fair: we have a collections system, an archive, published research, and specialists who know all of it. True — and every one of those is written in prose.

Prose can be read. It cannot be traversed. No amount of search will answer a question that has to cross four documents held by three departments in two vocabularies.

KM for Museums & Libraries · 01 — Where it stands today

The institutions with the best knowledge in the room are losing the room.

Four educated adults spend an afternoon in a well-funded new gallery, read every panel, try every interactive — and leave with nothing they can repeat to a child in the car. Meanwhile that same family's Sunday is being contested by a cinema, a shopping centre and a documentary made for more money than the gallery cost to fit out.

Neither problem is about money, staff or accuracy. Both are about form.

A library has the same afternoon and a different version of it. A reader arrives with one question that touches special collections, the archive and the digital repository, and leaves with three unrelated answers and no sense that the three are connected — because in the institution's own systems, they aren't.

The sentence every director should be afraid of
“I can't name one thing I understand now that I didn't understand this morning.”

Illustrative composite, drawn from patterns we see repeatedly. No institution is named — the pattern is near-universal, and singling one out would be both unfair and beside the point.

The failures were never about content quality. Stations of accurate information with nothing saying how any of them produced any other. Analogies borrowed from a landscape the visitor has never stood in. Three time-scales in three rooms, never reconciled. An exhibition that rewards only the visitor who already knew.

Every one of those is a failure of relationships between pieces of knowledge — precisely the thing nobody had modelled.

One method, two kinds of institution

The discipline is the same. What differs is which question keeps failing, and who fails to answer it.

In a museum

The question that crosses departments

A curator wants every object connected to one theme, one expedition, one maker. The answer exists — spread across ceramics, textiles, conservation and the archive, in four vocabularies.

The unit
An object, with an accession record and a chain of custody
The failing question
“What else in this building relates to this?”
Who is let down
The visitor at the label, and the curator planning next season
In a library

The enquiry that stops at the catalogue

A reader asks one question. The catalogue answers for published holdings, the archive for papers, the repository for digitised material — and nothing tells them the three describe the same subject.

The unit
A record, with authority control and a finding aid
The failing question
“What do we hold on this, in every form we hold it?”
Who is let down
The reader at the desk, and the subject librarian answering by memory

Same failure, different furniture. Both institutions hold the answer already; in both, the answer cannot travel. That is why one method serves both — and why the ontology has to be built from your vocabulary, not from the other sector's.

The uncomfortable diagnosis
They are not beating you on knowledge. They are beating you on structure.

A platform can recommend, sequence and adapt because everything it holds is structured from the moment it is made. Its advantage is not better material. It is worse material, organised properly.

Yours is incomparably richer and sits in prose — catalogue records, conservation files, exhibition scripts, and the memories of people approaching retirement. Rich, correct, and structurally unable to be delivered as an adaptive experience.

That gap is closeable — not by out-spending a studio, and not by bolting a chatbot onto a website.

KM for Museums & Libraries · 02 — What changes for the visitor and the reader

The holdings don't change. The paths through them do.

Once concepts, objects, people and evidence are modelled as a graph, a route through the collection becomes a question rather than a construction project — and can be recomposed endlessly from scholarship you already own. In a gallery that route is a visit. In a reading room it is an answer that finally crosses the catalogue, the archive and the digital repository in one move.

Same exhibit, same evening, three visitors
Standing in front of the same object

This is not three exhibitions. It is one concept, with depth held as a property and different relationships followed for each visitor. Illustrative example.

Experience 01

A different thread every visit

The permanent collection stays permanent — but the route through it can change monthly, entered through trade, through repair and reuse, through a single decade.

Experience 02

The stored collection joins in

Most of what you hold will never fit in a gallery. Connected, those objects still take part — as the thing the object in front of you turns out to be related to.

Experience 03

Every question becomes an asset

Questions the collection can't answer are recorded against the item. Repeated often enough, they become a research agenda ranked by real public curiosity.

Experience 04 · reading room

A reference answer that shows its working

A reader's enquiry returns your own holdings, your own finding aids and your own subject expertise — with the trail attached — instead of a ranked list that stops at whatever happens to be catalogued in one system.

And the guardrail that makes it safe to do at all
A visitor-facing AI is not an exhibit. It is publishing.

Every answer it gives is an institutional statement, made in your name, at scale, without a curator reading it first. An institution that holds society's knowledge cannot let statements without provenance go out under its name — and that obligation doesn't lapse because the statement was generated rather than written.

Which is why the layer comes first, and why the rules on it are strict: sensitive material stays inside your boundary, contested attributions stay contested, and only a curator promotes a claim to a fact. Prepared properly, the constraint becomes the feature — an AI that is structurally incapable of overclaiming is something no general assistant can offer.

KM for Museums & Libraries · 03 — What leadership looks like from here
The projection

Leadership in this era means being upstream.

The world is not short of answers any more. It is short of answers anyone can check. Every organisation now generating content at volume has the same problem, and none of them can solve it — because solving it requires exactly what your institution has been accumulating for a century.

The move is from being a destination people visit to being a source the world draws on.

Horizon 01 · your floor, your reading room

The collection answers, in your institution's voice

Visitors and readers ask, and receive your own scholarship at the depth they need, with the evidence attached. A return visit — or a return enquiry — offers what the last one didn't. The measure stops being footfall and issue counts, and becomes whether anyone left holding a question.

Horizon 02 · your people

Curators and librarians stop searching and start asking

The reasoning behind an attribution or a cataloguing decision is captured while the person who made it is still here to explain it. An exhibition question — or a reference enquiry that used to take weeks of hunting — is answered in an afternoon, across every department at once.

Horizon 03 · beyond the walls

Schools and researchers build on you directly

Rather than citing a web page, a curriculum team, a university or a peer institution queries your layer and gets sourced, current, attributable knowledge. You stop being a reference and become a supplier.

Horizon 04 · the AI ecosystem

You become the ground truth others reach for

When systems need verified knowledge in your domain, yours is the layer they query — and every answer they give carries your attribution. That is what leadership means now: not the loudest voice, but the one everyone else is standing on.

Why this is reachable, and reachable now
The expensive half was finished a century ago.

Nobody has to acquire the objects, establish the provenance, build the scholarship or earn the public's trust — that work is done, paid for, and irreplaceable. What remains is expressing it in a form the present century can use.

It does not begin with a platform purchase or an institution-wide programme. It begins with one question your specialists could not answer, modelled properly, end to end — and then your own team doing the next collection without us.

07 — Where we're honest with you

What this is not, and what it asks of your team.

We'd rather lose the engagement than have you discover this in month four.

Needs your specialists

Your specialists’ time is the real cost of the engagement

Not our fees — their calendars. Expect senior curators, librarians and archivists in working sessions on a regular rhythm. If that time can't be committed, the work will not hold.

Does not replace scholarship

Only your own specialists promote a claim to an established fact

The system holds claims and their evidence. It never adjudicates — a curator or a subject librarian does. Anyone telling you a machine can do that research is selling you a liability.

Not a quick fix

This is a design exercise before it is a technology one

The hard part is getting departments to agree on terminology they have each held privately for decades. No product shortcuts that.

Not a body shop

We won't run this as an outsourced digitisation project

If you want a firm to go away and return with a finished knowledge graph, we're the wrong partner — and that deliverable tends to be abandoned within a year anyway.

Deliberately narrow

We don't do institution-wide big-bang programmes

One collection, one enquiry, end to end — then widen. Modelling everything at once is how these projects die.
